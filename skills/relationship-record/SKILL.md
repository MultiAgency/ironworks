# Reasoning over a relationship record

This is how you get from a pile of dated activities to an answer. It is a way of reading, not a
procedure to narrate: do the work, then answer the question. Step 8 says what reaches the reply.

## 1. Fix the question first

Decide what is actually being asked before you read anything, because it decides what counts as
relevant. "What do we owe them?" and "what changed?" and "what needs my attention?" are three
different reads of the same record.

If the question names a relationship, work on that one. If it spans everything ("what needs
attention across all of them"), work across the records you were given and say how many you
looked at.

## 2. Read the record in time order

Put the activities in date order and read forward. The shape of a relationship is a sequence, and
almost every useful conclusion comes from comparing two dated entries rather than from reading
one.

Note as you go: what kind of event each is, who it involves, and what it would change.

## 3. Sort the evidence by what it can support

Map each piece to the tier your instructions define, by what it is:

- **A durable record** of something that happened — an agreement, a change to one, a payment, a
  report delivered, a meeting held, a decision taken → **FACT**, cited to it.
- **A recorded statement**, and **what someone says in the conversation right now** → **STATED**,
  attributed to the person and dated.
- **Your own reading of the above** → **INFERENCE**, with the basis named.

The upgrade from STATED to FACT requires a record. Plausible, repeated, and from someone senior
are each still STATED.

## 4. Derive the current state

Work forward through the sequence and let later evidence overwrite earlier evidence of the same
thing. What is current is what the most recent record bearing on it says.

State it as current, name the record it rests on, and name the date it changed. Where two records
disagree and neither supersedes the other, that is a contradiction — see step 6.

## 5. Find what is outstanding

A commitment is outstanding until something in the record closes it. For each commitment you
identified, ask what evidence would show it complete, and whether that evidence exists.

Place each in exactly one of three cases:

- **closed** — a later record shows it done;
- **outstanding** — nothing shows it done, and something should by now;
- **not yet due** — nothing shows it done, and nothing should yet.

For a recurring obligation, check each occurrence against the calendar rather than checking
whether it ever happened.

Then say **who it is with**: what is waiting on MultiAgency, and what is waiting on the
counterparty. That single split is often the whole answer.

## 6. Surface contradiction, staleness, and absence

- **Contradiction** — two records that cannot both be true. Quote both, date both, and do not
  pick a winner. Naming it is the value.
- **Staleness** — the last activity is old enough that its absence is itself a signal. Say the
  date and say what was expected, or it is just an observation about a calendar.
- **Absence** — a claim the record does not carry. Say what is missing, why it matters, and what
  would settle it. Where the answer to the question turns on it, say the answer is UNKNOWN and
  stop; do not reach for the nearest supported thing instead.

Report only the absences that bear on the question asked.

## 7. Say what needs a person

Some things you can only surface: a decision to be taken, a conversation to be had, a figure to
be reconciled, a commitment to renegotiate. Name it, say why it needs a human, and say what you
would want to know before it is taken.

Never present this as a task list, a workflow, or a set of next actions to be executed. You are
naming judgement calls, not assigning work.

## 8. Answer

Lead with the finding, in the first sentence — never with what you are about to do. Put the
evidence under it, each claim carrying its tag and its citation.

Then cut. Steps 2 to 7 are how you *read*; they are not an outline to write to, and an answer
with a section per step is this skill leaking into the reply. Keep what bears on the question and
what would change a decision, say each thing once, and stop when the question is answered.

If the honest answer is "the record does not say", that is the answer. Give it first, then say
what the record *does* establish nearby, then what would settle it.
